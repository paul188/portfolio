## Overview

This is an automated **subdomain-takeover scanner** for white-hat bug-bounty
research. It continuously walks the public scope of programs on **HackerOne,
Bugcrowd, Intigriti, and YesWeHack**, enumerates each domain's subdomains, and
classifies which of them carry a **dangling DNS record** — a record that still
points at a third-party resource the owner no longer controls, and which an
attacker could therefore re-claim.

The architecture follows the taxonomy and detection logic of the foundational
academic paper on the subject — [*All Your DNS Records Point to Us: Understanding
the Security Threats of Dangling DNS Records*](https://shhaos.github.io/papers/ccs16-dares.pdf)
(Liu, Hao & Wang, **ACM CCS 2016**) — and adapts it to the bug-bounty setting,
including the crucial update of *which* of the paper's record classes are still
exploitable after a decade of cloud-provider mitigations.

It is a respectful, rate-limited scanner: it only touches assets that are
explicitly in-scope on public VDP programs, skips `.gov` and `.mil`, identifies
itself with a contactable User-Agent, and performs **detection only** — the
exploitation PoC is a separate, manually-invoked tool.

## 1. Background: Dangling DNS Records (the DARE paper)

The CCS '16 paper coined the term **"Dare"** (dangling DNS record) and is the
reference taxonomy this scanner is built around. A Dare arises whenever a domain
keeps a DNS record pointing to a resource it has stopped controlling — an
abandoned cloud service, a released IP, or a deleted hosted zone. The paper
classifies them by record type, and the scanner mirrors that classification:

**What comes directly from the paper, and why:**
- The **NS-delegation detector** is a faithful implementation of the paper's
  dangling-NS attack: query the delegated nameservers *directly* for the zone's
  `SOA`; a `SERVFAIL`/`NXDOMAIN` from the provider means the hosted zone no longer
  exists, so the delegation is dangling.
- The **CNAME-chain + service-fingerprint** path implements the paper's
  dangling-CNAME class: resolve the full CNAME chain, then decide whether the
  final target is an *unclaimed* third-party resource (e.g. an `NXDOMAIN` on a
  service that requires the resource to exist, or a "no such bucket" signature).
- The **zone-claim PoC** (`claim_zone.py`) realises the paper's exploitation
  primitive for dangling NS — re-creating the hosted zone at the provider to
  obtain authoritative control of the victim zone.

## 2. Threat Models

The reason a dangling record matters is that controlling a victim's (sub)domain
is far more dangerous than hosting look-alike content elsewhere. The paper
enumerates the attack surface a successful takeover unlocks; the scanner exists to
find these *before* an attacker does:

## 3. Program & Scope Discovery

Program lists are pulled from the community-maintained **bounty-targets-data**
repository (refreshed every six hours), covering all four platforms with no API
keys required. Each scope entry — wildcards (`*.example.com`), full URLs, or bare
domains — is normalised to a plain apex domain; IPs, single-label names, and
out-of-scope TLDs (`.gov`, `.mil`) are dropped. Programs are stored in SQLite and
re-scanned on a rolling 24-hour cadence.

---

## 4. Subdomain Enumeration & Wildcard Filtering

For each apex, subdomains are gathered from five passive sources — **crt.sh,
Wayback Machine, AlienVault OTX, Anubis, and HackerTarget** — so no intrusive
brute-forcing is needed. Before analysis, each apex is probed for **wildcard
DNS**: two random non-existent subdomains are resolved, and if both return the
same IP, that IP is recorded and used to suppress the false positives wildcard
records would otherwise generate.

---

## 5. CNAME Takeover Detection

Candidate subdomains run through a two-stage detector:

1. **CNAME-chain resolution** — the chain is followed recursively (depth-limited),
   distinguishing a live target, an `NXDOMAIN` endpoint, and a resolver error.
2. **Service fingerprinting** — the final CNAME target is matched against **70+
   service signatures** (S3, Elastic Beanstalk, Azure, Heroku, Shopify, Fastly,
   Vercel, Netlify, …). Some services are only takeable when the endpoint returns
   `NXDOMAIN`; the candidate logic encodes that per-service condition.

Confirmation uses two complementary engines: **nuclei** with its `takeover`
templates (filtered to high/critical), plus a **Python HTTP fallback** that checks
body signatures for services that have no nuclei template. A state-transition
guard suppresses re-alerts for already-known findings to keep the signal clean.

---

## 6. NS-Delegation Detection & the Zone-Claim PoC

`ns_checker.py` implements the paper's dangling-NS attack as a detector. For every
apex and every non-resolving subdomain it:

1. Queries the `NS` records and checks whether they belong to a tracked cloud DNS
   provider (Azure DNS, Google Cloud DNS, DigitalOcean, NS1, Hetzner).
2. Resolves those nameservers and queries **each one directly** for the zone's
   `SOA`.
3. Declares the zone dangling only when **at least two** nameservers independently
   return `SERVFAIL`/`NXDOMAIN` — a deliberately conservative rule to avoid
   false positives from transient timeouts.

The matching exploitation tool, `claim_zone.py`, demonstrates the takeover for
authorised targets: it repeatedly creates a hosted zone at the provider until the
randomly-assigned nameserver set matches the victim's delegation, then drops a
signed `_bugbounty-poc` TXT record as proof — and is built to clean up every
unmatched zone it creates.

---

## 7. Knowing What Not to Report

The most important engineering decision in the project is **negative**: the paper's
2016 taxonomy lists classes that cloud providers have since hardened, and blindly
reporting them produces invalid, noisy submissions. Two whole classes are
deliberately **excluded** from the detector:

  <thead><tr style={{borderBottom:'2px solid var(--neutral-border-medium)'}}>
    <th style={{padding:'6px 10px',textAlign:'left'}}>Excluded class</th>
    <th style={{padding:'6px 10px',textAlign:'left'}}>Why it is no longer exploitable</th>
  </tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr style={{borderBottom:'1px solid var(--neutral-border-weak)'}}>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',fontWeight:600}}>Route53 dangling NS</td>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',color:'var(--neutral-on-background-weak)'}}>AWS added protections (2023) preventing NS reassignment for deleted hosted zones, so the zone-claim attack no longer executes; programs close these as informative</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',fontWeight:600}}>GitHub Pages CNAME</td>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',color:'var(--neutral-on-background-weak)'}}>Organisation-level custom-domain verification (a `_github-challenge` TXT record) blocks anyone else from claiming a verified domain, making the CNAME path non-exploitable in practice</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

This is the difference between a scanner that *measures* dangling records and one
that surfaces *actionable* ones. Combined with the wildcard filter, the
multi-resolver checks, and the conservative two-nameserver NS rule, the goal is a
low-false-positive feed — reporting only what a triager can reproduce. (Dangling-A
detection is likewise left out: re-acquiring a specific released cloud IP is rarely
reliably exploitable and is poorly received by programs.)

---

## 8. Engineering & Operational Design

- **Fully asynchronous** — `asyncio` + `aiohttp` with per-stage semaphores (DNS 50,
  enumeration 15, HTTP 20, NS 10) so thousands of lookups run concurrently under a
  global rate limit.
- **Idempotent state** — every subdomain, DNS result, and finding is upserted into
  SQLite; re-scans refresh rows rather than duplicating, and dead hosts are aged
  out after repeated failures.
- **Continuous & resumable** — a long-running loop refreshes programs every 6 h,
  re-scans each every 24 h, and shuts down gracefully on `SIGINT`/`SIGTERM`.
- **Respectful by construction** — four public resolvers, a contactable
  User-Agent, in-scope assets only, and `.gov`/`.mil` skipped.
