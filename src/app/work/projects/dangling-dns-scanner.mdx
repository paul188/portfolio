## Overview

An automated subdomain-takeover scanner I run for white-hat bug-bounty research.
It works through the public scope of programs on HackerOne, Bugcrowd, Intigriti
and YesWeHack, enumerates their subdomains, and flags any that carry a dangling
DNS record: a record still pointing at a third-party resource the owner has given
up, which someone else can re-claim.

The detection logic comes from the paper that first characterised the problem,
[*All Your DNS Records Point to Us*](https://shhaos.github.io/papers/ccs16-dares.pdf)
(Liu, Hao & Wang, ACM CCS 2016). I took its taxonomy and adapted it to a
bug-bounty workflow, then updated it for a question the paper predates: which of
its record classes are still exploitable now that the big cloud providers have
hardened against them.

The scanner is built to stay polite. It only looks at assets a program lists as
in scope, skips `.gov` and `.mil`, sends a User-Agent that points back to me, and
does detection only. The proof-of-concept that actually claims a zone is a
separate tool, run by hand.

## 1. Background: Dangling DNS Records (the DARE paper)

The paper coined the term *dangling DNS record* (shortened to *Dare*), and its
taxonomy is what the scanner is organised around. A record dangles when a domain
keeps pointing a DNS entry at a resource it no longer controls: an abandoned
cloud service, a released IP, a deleted hosted zone. The paper sorts these by
record type, and the scanner uses the same split.

Which parts come straight from the paper:

- The NS-delegation detector implements the paper's dangling-NS attack. It queries
  the delegated nameservers directly for the zone's `SOA`; a `SERVFAIL` or
  `NXDOMAIN` from the provider means the hosted zone is gone, so the delegation is
  dangling.
- The CNAME-chain and fingerprint path covers the dangling-CNAME class. It
  resolves the full CNAME chain, then decides whether the final target is an
  unclaimed third-party resource (an `NXDOMAIN` on a service that needs the
  resource to exist, or a "no such bucket" type of signature).
- The zone-claim PoC (`claim_zone.py`) is the paper's exploitation step in
  practice: re-create the hosted zone at the provider and take authoritative
  control of the victim zone.

## 2. Threat Models

A dangling record matters because taking over a victim's own subdomain is worse
than standing up a look-alike somewhere else. The paper lays out what a takeover
gives an attacker; finding those cases first is the whole point of the scanner.

## 3. Program & Scope Discovery

Program lists come from the community-maintained bounty-targets-data repository,
refreshed every six hours, which covers all four platforms without API keys. Each
scope entry is normalised to a plain apex domain, whether it arrives as a wildcard
(`*.example.com`), a full URL, or a bare domain. IPs, single-label names and
out-of-scope TLDs like `.gov` and `.mil` are dropped. Programs live in SQLite and
are re-scanned every 24 hours.

---

## 4. Subdomain Enumeration & Wildcard Filtering

Subdomains for each apex come from five passive sources (crt.sh, the Wayback
Machine, AlienVault OTX, Anubis and HackerTarget), so there is no intrusive
brute-forcing. Each apex is also checked for wildcard DNS first: the scanner
resolves two random non-existent subdomains, and if both return the same IP it
records that IP and uses it to filter out the false positives a wildcard would
otherwise produce.

---

## 5. CNAME Takeover Detection

Candidate subdomains go through two stages:

1. CNAME-chain resolution. The chain is followed recursively up to a depth limit,
   separating a live target, an `NXDOMAIN` endpoint, and a resolver error.
2. Service fingerprinting. The final CNAME target is matched against 70+ service
   signatures (S3, Elastic Beanstalk, Azure, Heroku, Shopify, Fastly, Vercel,
   Netlify, and so on). Some services can only be taken over when the endpoint
   returns `NXDOMAIN`, and the candidate logic encodes that per service.

Confirmation runs two engines side by side: nuclei with its `takeover` templates
(limited to high and critical), and a Python HTTP fallback that checks body
signatures for the services nuclei has no template for. A state-transition guard
suppresses repeat alerts for findings already seen, so the feed stays readable.

---

## 6. NS-Delegation Detection & the Zone-Claim PoC

`ns_checker.py` runs the dangling-NS check. For every apex, and every subdomain
that does not resolve, it:

1. Queries the `NS` records and checks whether they belong to a tracked cloud DNS
   provider (Azure DNS, Google Cloud DNS, DigitalOcean, NS1, Hetzner).
2. Resolves those nameservers and asks each one directly for the zone's `SOA`.
3. Calls the zone dangling only when at least two nameservers independently return
   `SERVFAIL` or `NXDOMAIN`. Requiring two keeps a transient timeout from being
   mistaken for a deleted zone.

The companion tool, `claim_zone.py`, proves the takeover on authorised targets. It
creates hosted zones at the provider until the randomly assigned nameserver set
matches the victim's delegation, then writes a signed `_bugbounty-poc` TXT record
as evidence. Any zone it creates that does not match is cleaned up.

## 7. Knowing What Not to Report

Some of the most useful work here was deciding what to leave out. Parts of the
2016 taxonomy have since been hardened by the cloud providers, and reporting them
anyway just generates invalid, noisy submissions. Two classes are excluded from
the detector on purpose:

  <thead><tr style={{borderBottom:'2px solid var(--neutral-border-medium)'}}>
    <th style={{padding:'6px 10px',textAlign:'left'}}>Excluded class</th>
    <th style={{padding:'6px 10px',textAlign:'left'}}>Why it is no longer exploitable</th>
  </tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr style={{borderBottom:'1px solid var(--neutral-border-weak)'}}>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',fontWeight:600}}>Route53 dangling NS</td>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',color:'var(--neutral-on-background-weak)'}}>AWS added protections (2023) preventing NS reassignment for deleted hosted zones, so the zone-claim attack no longer executes; programs close these as informative</td>
    </tr>
    <tr>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',fontWeight:600}}>GitHub Pages CNAME</td>
      <td style={{padding:'6px 10px',color:'var(--neutral-on-background-weak)'}}>Organisation-level custom-domain verification (a `_github-challenge` TXT record) blocks anyone else from claiming a verified domain, making the CNAME path non-exploitable in practice</td>
    </tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

That distinction, between measuring dangling records and surfacing the ones worth
reporting, is the point. The wildcard filter, the multiple resolvers and the
two-nameserver rule all push the same way: keep the false-positive rate low enough
that everything the scanner surfaces is something a triager can reproduce.
Dangling-A detection is left out for the same reason, since re-acquiring one
specific released cloud IP is rarely reliable and programs tend to reject it.

## 8. Engineering & Operational Design

- Asynchronous throughout: `asyncio` and `aiohttp` with per-stage semaphores
  (DNS 50, enumeration 15, HTTP 20, NS 10), so thousands of lookups run at once
  under a global rate limit.
- Idempotent storage: every subdomain, DNS result and finding is upserted into
  SQLite, so re-scans refresh rows instead of duplicating them, and dead hosts age
  out after repeated failures.
- Continuous and resumable: a long-running loop refreshes the program list every
  6 hours, re-scans each program every 24 hours, and shuts down cleanly on
  `SIGINT`/`SIGTERM`.
- Polite by default: four public resolvers, a contactable User-Agent, in-scope
  assets only, `.gov` and `.mil` skipped.
