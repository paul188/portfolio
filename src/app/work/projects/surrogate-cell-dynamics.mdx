## Overview

Simulating how cells communicate through cytokines requires solving a reaction–diffusion PDE on complex 3D geometries. With finite-element methods this takes seconds to minutes per sample. This project trains a **neural surrogate model** that maps a spatial cell configuration to the steady-state cytokine concentration on each cell's surface, reducing inference to milliseconds. The application is the tumour microenvironment (InterpretTME), where signalling between immune and tumour cells determines treatment response.

---

## 1. Governing PDEs

Cytokine transport in the extracellular space follows a **quasi-steady-state reaction–diffusion equation**:

<BlockMath math="D \,\Delta c(x) - \eta\, c(x) = 0 \qquad x \in \Omega" />

where <InlineMath math="c(x)" /> is the cytokine concentration, <InlineMath math="D" /> the diffusion coefficient and <InlineMath math="\eta" /> the decay rate.

Secreting cells emit cytokine at a prescribed flux on their surface. Absorbing cells take it up through their receptors, modelled by a nonlinear Michaelis-Menten uptake function:

<BlockMath math="\psi(c,\, R) \;=\; \frac{k_{\text{endo}}\cdot R \cdot c}{\dfrac{k_{\text{off}}}{k_{\text{on}}} + c}" />

Here <InlineMath math="R" /> is the receptor count per cell, <InlineMath math="k_{\text{endo}}" /> the endocytosis rate constant, and <InlineMath math="k_{\text{off}}/k_{\text{on}}" /> the effective dissociation constant. At high concentrations the receptors saturate and uptake plateaus. This nonlinearity makes the problem hard to approximate analytically.

## 2. FEM Data Generation

Ground-truth labels come from solving the PDE with the **finite-element method (FEM)**. Each sample places **N = 50 spherical cells** at random in a cubic domain, assigns each a type and a receptor count R, and averages the solved concentration over each cell's surface. **Data augmentation:** the concentration field is invariant under axis reflections and rotations by multiples of <InlineMath math="\pi/2" />, which multiplies the effective dataset size without additional FEM solves. The trained model achieves a final loss of **~1 × 10⁻³** on held-out configurations.

## 3. Model Selection

The model input is a **point cloud**: an unordered set of N cell positions with per-point features (type, receptor count). The output is one concentration per point. Three approaches were considered.

## 4. The PointNet Architecture

A modified PointNet (diagram at the top of the page) with four design choices driven by the physics of the problem.

### Rotation-Invariant Input Encoding

Instead of raw cell coordinates, an **autoencoder maps the <InlineMath math="\frac{n(n-1)}{2}" /> pairwise inter-cell distances** into a latent space of dimension <InlineMath math="3N - 6" />. This removes the 3 translational and 3 rotational degrees of freedom, so the representation is invariant under rigid motions of the whole system.

### Distance-Based Attention

Influence between cells is weighted by:

<BlockMath math="\text{softmax}\!\left(-\|x_i - x_j\|^2\right)" />

Cells further away receive exponentially smaller weights, mirroring the spatial decay of concentration fields.

### Mean Pooling over Max Pooling

Standard PointNet uses max pooling. Here **mean pooling** is used, because the target is the *average* surface concentration, which depends on contributions from all cells.

### Skip Connections and Decoder

Skip connections let local per-cell features bypass the global pooling step. The decoder is an MLP on the concatenated global and local features:

<BlockMath math="2048 \;\to\; 1024 \;\to\; 512 \;\to\; 256 \;\to\; 128 \;\to\; 64 \;\to\; 1" />

It outputs the predicted mean cytokine concentration on each cell's surface. The same weights are applied to every point, which preserves permutation invariance.
